## 1 Introduction

The United States will keep its growth lead over Europe through 2031: the IMF expects 2.3% real growth for the US in 2026 and 2027 against 1.1% and 1.2% for the euro area (IMF, 2026). The usual explanation, that Europe is uniformly over-taxed and over-regulated, is wrong in one respect. Europe is two models sharing a currency, and the average of the two describes neither.

The first model, Austria, Germany, France, Belgium, the Netherlands and the Nordics, combines high income with high charges on labour, expensive energy and dense administration. An Austrian owner distributing 1 million euros of profit keeps about 558,000 euros, and the top income-tax rate of 55% runs to the end of 2029 (Tax Foundation, 2026). The second model, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary, Poland and the Baltics, runs flat taxes of 10% to 16%, labour at 12 to 20 euros an hour and output per head at 68% to 90% of the EU average (Eurostat, 2026a). Averaged, the EU shows a corporate rate of 21.6% against 25.6% in the US; no firm pays it. The article therefore compares pairs: Austria and Germany with Massachusetts, New York and California on tax structure; Romania and Bulgaria with Mississippi and West Virginia on income; Estonia and Ireland with Texas and Florida as low-tax locations.

The argument runs in four parts. Part I measures the gap and finds that it is a gap in dynamics, not in levels. Part II explains why the European core is expensive: a state that spends more than half of output, a tax stack that falls on labour rather than capital, energy at three to four times the Texan price, and rules that are honest but slow. Part III follows the consequence: thin risk capital, a cautious investment climate, a technology market in which US firms took about 75% of global AI venture capital in 2025 against 6% for the EU-27 (OECD, 2026a), and a talent flow that has begun to reverse. Part IV turns these forces into scenarios to 2031.

## Part I: The Gap

### 2 Size, Income and Growth

In current dollars the US economy is about 1.4 times the EU's, in purchasing-power parities about 1.06 times: IMF projections for 2026 put nominal GDP at 32.4 trillion dollars for the US and 23.0 trillion for the EU (IMF via StatisticsTimes, 2026). Per head the US leads by about 85% nominally and 39% at purchasing power; Eurostat puts EU GDP per head at 41,566 euros in purchasing power standards (PPS) for 2025 (Eurostat, 2026a). Two caveats: Irish GDP per head stands at 237% of the EU average while its consumption per head is at the average, since multinational profit-booking inflates both figures (Eurostat, 2026b), and US GDP counts health spending that produces no better outcomes.

US states spread as widely as EU members: BEA nominal GDP per head for 2025 is about 123,000 dollars in New York, 114,000 in Massachusetts, 109,000 in California, 90,000 nationally, 62,000 in West Virginia and 55,000 in Mississippi (BEA, 2026). On World Bank purchasing-power data Austria stood at 63,970 dollars in 2025, Romania at about 41,000 and Bulgaria at about 35,000, while the IMF's October 2026 outlook gives Austria about 76,000; Switzerland reaches about 85,700. Three pairs follow: Romania and Bulgaria match Mississippi and West Virginia on income; Austria and Germany match Massachusetts and California on tax and cost structure but sit closer to Kentucky or Alabama on income; only Luxembourg, Ireland and Switzerland match the strongest US states. An Austrian entrepreneur thus carries a California-grade burden on a below-average American income base. Section 3 asks how much of that gap is productivity rather than price level.

### 3 Productivity: A Stable Level, a Widening Dynamic

The level gap has been stable for 25 years; the dynamics gap has not. US output per hour has risen 9.7% since 2019, EU output 2.4%, and in 2024 the two stood at 116 and 72 dollars per hour in current purchasing-power parities (World Employment Confederation, 2025). The OECD reports 84.1 dollars for the US and 135.7 for Ireland in constant 2020 parities, noting that a gross-national-income measure would place Ireland near the other high-income European economies (OECD, 2026b).

Three readings of the gap exist. ITIF attributes 72% of the US lead of 34% at purchasing power to lower productivity per hour and 28% to fewer hours (ITIF, 2025). The Banque de France found the hours gap narrowed from about 20% in 2000 to 10% in 2023, with shorter working time still the largest factor in Germany and France (Banque de France, 2025). Bruegel and Krugman hold the level ratio constant over 25 years, with the eastern members rising from 32% to 55% of US income per head between 1995 and 2022 (Bruegel, 2025). A 2026 Richmond Fed brief reconciles them: levels stable, growth lag real (Richmond Fed, 2026). This article follows that reading, since the lag concentrates in software and AI \[PLACEHOLDER: OECD GDP per hour worked 2024 for AT, DE, FR, NL, SE, PL, RO\].

Research intensity in the core is high but uneven: only Sweden (3.6%), Belgium (3.4%), Austria (3.3%), Finland (3.2%), Germany (3.1%) and Denmark (3.0%) spend 3% or more of GDP on R&D, against 2.24% for the EU in 2024 and about 3.5% for the US on older OECD data (Eurostat, 2025) \[PLACEHOLDER: OECD MSTI 2026\]. Austria's research base is a real strength that capital, energy and labour costs make hard to monetise. Part II prices those costs, starting with what the state spends.

The ratio buys different things. In Austria and Germany it covers health insurance for dependants without extra premium, a statutory pension, severance provision and fee-free university study; a US employer buys health cover privately at 9,325 dollars for a single person and 26,993 for a family in 2025, with employees paying 16% and 26% (KFF, 2025) \[PLACEHOLDER: Eurostat gov\_10a\_exp, functional breakdown 2024\].

Austria and France gain room only after 2028. Section 5 shows how that spending is collected and who bears it.

A no-income-tax US state delivers about 1.4 times the net pay per euro of labour cost of Austria or Germany, even after private health insurance. OECD Taxing Wages 2026 confirms it: the tax wedge on a single average earner is 52.5% in Belgium, 49.3% in Germany, 47.2% in France and 47.1% in Austria against 35.1% for the OECD and about 30% in the US, and Austria's marginal wedge is 58.3% (OECD, 2026d).

Verdict: owner-level capital tax is not the core's distinctive problem, since Austria's 44% sits near the US population-weighted average of 47% and below California; labour taxes are, at 15 to 20 points above the US with no state close; the top rates of 55% in Austria and France and 60.5% in Denmark punish founders who take salary; and the missing pass-through regime is the largest structural gap for SMEs. In Central and Eastern Europe the constraint is income, capital depth and, in Romania, fiscal instability, not tax. The tax stack is only part of the operating cost; energy and labour add the rest.

### 6 What Else a Firm Pays: Energy and Labour

Industrial electricity in Germany, Austria, Denmark and Italy costs three to four times the Texan price; in Sweden and Finland it costs less than in California. Eurostat's second-half 2025 non-household price (band IC, excluding recoverable taxes) was 18.37 cents per kilowatt-hour for the EU, 22.64 in Germany, 25.52 in Ireland and 7.48 in Finland (Eurostat, 2026d); the all-in 2025 ranking puts Ireland, Germany, Denmark, Hungary and Italy at 26 to 29 cents, Austria at 24.1, Romania 21.6, France 18.9, Bulgaria 16.1, Sweden 12.1 and Finland 9.6 (Apiar, 2026). EIA industrial prices for January–June 2026 are 8.89 US cents nationally, 6.65 in Texas, 7.09 in Washington and 19.88 in California (EIA, 2026); the IEA puts EU energy-intensive industry above twice the US level and ACER puts EU gas at three times the US price (IEA, 2026; ACER, 2026).

The 2026 Hormuz shock widened the gap where it was widest. After the strikes on Iran on 28 February 2026 the TTF rose almost 50% to 47.94 euros per megawatt-hour on 3 March and reached 80.32 euros on 11 September, Henry Hub rose about 13% in the first week against 70% for TTF, and euro-area energy inflation hit 14.3% in August (CEPR, 2026; CNBC, 2026). Germany, Italy, Austria and Hungary are most exposed; Sweden, Finland and France least.

Bulgaria, Hungary and Romania sit at 40 to 45, a range Transparency International classes as a serious problem, so the periphery's low tax rates carry a legal-certainty discount.

On competitiveness the order reverses. The indices capture rules, not execution, which is where Austria's burden lies: compulsory Chamber of Commerce membership with a levy, licensing under the Gewerbeordnung, employer charges of about 29.5% on every gross wage (social insurance 20.98%, severance fund 1.53%, family fund 3.7% plus surcharge, municipal payroll tax 3%; LexisNexis, 2026), and a record 6,857 insolvencies in 2025, 19 a day, concentrated in trade, construction and hospitality (KSV1870, 2026). Germany adds compulsory chamber membership, trade tax and slow permitting (DIHK, 2025).

Brussels has conceded the point three times. Only 15.7% of the Draghi report's 383 recommendations were fully implemented by July 2026, 41.3% counting partial progress (EPIC, 2026). The Digital Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) postponed the AI Act's high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028 (European Commission, 2026a). EU Inc. (COM/2026/321), an optional EU-wide company form with a pan-EU employee share scheme, received the Council's position on 23 October 2026 with the share scheme kept and tax harmonisation dropped (Council of the European Union, 2026).

The American alternative is regulation by court ruling with a short lifespan. The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on 20 February 2026 after 175 billion dollars had been collected, cutting the trade-weighted average tariff from 15.3% to 8.3% (Global Trade Alert, 2026); a Section 122 surcharge replaced them within days, expired on 24 July, and gave way the same day to an uncapped Section 301 action covering more than 80 economies (Yale Budget Lab, 2026). The EU's 15% ceiling has applied since 1 July 2026, with steel and aluminium at 50% \[PLACEHOLDER: Official Journal\]. European investors cannot lose a trade regime overnight; American investors can. Part III follows where capital and people go under these conditions.

## Part III: Where Capital and Talent Go

### 8 Capital Availability

Europe lacks risk capital, not founders, because its savings sit in banks, insurance and pay-as-you-go entitlements rather than equity. Pension assets exceeded GDP at end-2024 in Denmark (206.4%), Switzerland (166.9%), the US (153.3%), the Netherlands (150.9%) and Sweden (115.8%), while Germany holds about 7% of GDP (OECD, 2025a; Apollo, 2026a); US pension and household investment assets reach almost 475% of GDP and market capitalisation over 200%, with Germany, France, Italy and Spain far below (Apollo, 2026b) \[PLACEHOLDER: Austria pension assets, % of GDP\].

The consequence is a self-sustaining venture gap. European pension funds allocate 0.018% of assets to venture capital against 1.9% in the US (Jacques Delors Centre, 2024), contributed only 0.6 billion euros or 5% of new European venture fund capital in 2023 (CEPS-ECMI, 2025), and European funds return 8.6% a year against 14.6% (CEPR, 2025), so European venture volume stays at about 22% of the US level.

Two rules reinforce it. Pillar Two now exempts US-parented groups while European groups face the full regime (EY, 2026), and national employee-equity schemes (Austria's 2024 start-up scheme, Germany's section 19a, France's BSPCE, Sweden's QESO) stop at the border and trail US incentive stock options and QSBS \[PLACEHOLDER: 2026 parameters\]. That is why the pan-EU share scheme in EU Inc. matters more than any rate cut.

The core's Mittelstand is built for this structure: bank-financed family firms that fail less often and scale less often. Austria's 2025 insolvency wave hit trade, construction and hospitality, not technology (KSV1870, 2026); European insolvency regimes are slower than Chapter 11; and the New York listings of Arm, Spotify, Klarna and UiPath show where pension money, option regimes and cost of capital align \[PLACEHOLDER: Eurostat SBS; ECB SAFE 2026; EIB Investment Survey 2025; Savings and Investments Union status\]. Thin capital shows in investor sentiment, which Section 9 measures.

### 9 Investment Climate in Autumn 2026

Sentiment is subdued on both sides in autumn 2026, for different reasons. The euro-area Economic Sentiment Indicator fell to 97.9 in September from 98.4, its first decline since April, on oil prices and the stalled talks to end the US–Iran war; July country values were 97.2 for Germany, 95.9 for France, 99.6 for Italy and 104.3 for Spain (European Commission, 2026b) \[PLACEHOLDER: ESI, ifo and WIFO October 2026\]. In the US the ISM manufacturing index stood at 54.5 in September with new orders at 55.3 (ISM, 2026), while NFIB small-business optimism fell to 97.9, just below its 52-year average, with uncertainty at 90 against a mean of 68 and labour availability (26%) and inflation (20%) the top problems (NFIB, 2026). Industry invests; small firms wait.

Venture capital is where the temperature differs. US investors deployed a record 515.8 billion dollars in the first three quarters of 2026, 82.7% in AI, with OpenAI and Anthropic raising over 200 billion in the first half (PitchBook-NVCA, 2026); Europe raised 44 billion euros in the first half, up 27% on an annual basis, 60.3% in AI (PitchBook, 2026), and Dealroom expects a record 10.5 billion dollars for European defence start-ups (Defense News, 2026). The IMF flags the concentration as a risk: if AI productivity disappoints, US GDP ends 1.8% below baseline by 2028 (IMF, 2026).

Europe's investment need of 1.2 trillion euros a year (Draghi, 2025) has no matching volume; German fiscal expansion and defence demand are the only sources that can narrow it within five years \[PLACEHOLDER: GFCF 2025 by country; UNCTAD WIR 2026; EIB survey 2025\]. Where the capital that does flow ends up is the subject of Section 10.

### 10 The Technology Market by Country

US firms took about 75% (194 billion dollars) of global AI venture capital in 2025, the EU-27 about 6% (15.8 billion) (OECD, 2026a). European venture totals for 2025 differ by method, not magnitude: 66.2 billion euros (PitchBook), 72 billion (Tech.eu), 85.3 billion dollars (KPMG), against about 339 billion dollars in the US with 65.6% in AI. The UK led Europe with 21.5 billion euros, then Germany 11.5, France 8.7 and Sweden about 3.2 billion dollars (Tech.eu, 2026; Dealroom, 2026); the largest 2026 rounds were Nscale (2.9 billion euros), Isomorphic Labs (2.1 billion dollars) and Neura Robotics (1.4 billion euros).

Capital concentrates in a few clusters. Paris: Mistral raised 1.7 billion euros at 11.7 billion in September 2025 and 3 billion at over 21 billion in September 2026, Europe's largest equity round. Munich: Celonis (1.8 billion dollars total), Helsing (1.8 billion Series E at 18 billion, July 2026), and Germany's 3.5 billion dollars of defence venture capital, now 15% of the EU total. Stockholm: Lovable, Legora, Klarna and Spotify, the EU's highest unicorn density. Amsterdam: ASML and Adyen. The second circle is Austria (Bitpanda, GoStudent, TTTech; an order of magnitude below Munich, no foundation-model cluster, the highest labour costs in its peer group \[PLACEHOLDER: AT venture volume 2025/H1 2026\]), Estonia (Bolt, Wise; zero tax on retained profit), Romania (UiPath now US-based; Bitdefender; IT tax exemption removed in 2025) and Poland (the largest engineering pool and nearshoring hub).

Infrastructure follows the same pattern. US hyperscalers hold about 70% of Europe's cloud market, European providers 15%, down from 29% in 2017 (Synergy Research, 2026). Intel cancelled its 30-billion-euro Magdeburg fab on 24 July 2025; ESMC Dresden (TSMC 70%; 28–12 nm; 40,000 wafers a month) targets production in late 2027; a Chips Act 2.0 (COM(2026) 504) awaits adoption in 2027. Europe attracts mature nodes, not frontier logic. Defence is the exception: European military spending rose 14% to 864 billion dollars in 2025, Germany 24% to 114 billion, while US spending fell 7.5% (SIPRI, 2026). The AI Act's high-risk rules are deferred to 2027/28; US federal AI policy is deregulatory. Companies form in Europe and scale or list in the US for the reasons Sections 5, 6 and 8 quantified. People move along the same routes, with one reversal that Section 11 describes.

### 11 Migration and Talent

Europe now takes in more people than the US. EU net migration was 2.05 million in 2025, offsetting a natural decline of 1.35 million (Eurostat, 2026f); US net immigration was 410,000 in calendar 2025 on CBO figures, 1.3 million for July 2024–June 2025 on Census figures after 2.7 million the year before, and about 321,000 projected to mid-2026, possibly turning negative (CBO, 2026; Census Bureau, 2026).

The US has made itself costlier for skilled entrants. A 100,000-dollar fee on certain H-1B petitions has applied since 21 September 2025, was vacated and blocked by federal courts in June and September 2026, yet was extended by proclamation to September 2027 pending appeal, and the lottery has been wage-weighted since 27 February 2026 (TheJobSeek, 2026) \[PLACEHOLDER: USCIS H-1B approvals FY2026\]. Europe's counter-offer, Choose Europe for Science with nearly 900 million euros by January 2026 and more than 100 national schemes (European Commission, 2026c), plus Germany's Chancenkarte and the reformed Blue Card, is blunted by language, salaries and slow administration; Austria's Red-White-Red Card competes with a 47% tax wedge against about 23% in Switzerland and 30% in the US \[PLACEHOLDER: card volumes 2025/26\].

Inside Europe, skilled workers move west while firms move work east to Bucharest, Cluj, Kraków, Brno and Sofia; inside the US, people and capital move from California and New York to Texas and Florida while frontier AI stays in California. Demographics settle the European question: the old-age dependency ratio of 34.5% in 2025 rises to about 60% by 2100 (Eurostat, 2026g), so immigration is a necessity, and the open question is whether Europe attracts engineers or only carers. Part IV combines these forces into scenarios.

## Part IV: Outlook

### 12 Forecast to 2031

The likely outcome is managed divergence: the US keeps frontier AI and the hyperscaler layer, Europe holds 20% to 30% of US venture volume and builds strength in defence, industrial AI and sovereignty niches, and the growth gap of about one point a year persists. Four factors can shift it: a US AI-investment correction, US immigration restrictions, European energy prices, and whether Brussels delivers EU Inc. and capital-market integration.

Institutional projections: IMF (October 2026) 2.3% US growth in 2026 and 2027, 1.1% and 1.2% for the euro area, 1.3% for Germany and 0.4% for France in 2026; CBO about 1.8% US growth after 2026 with deficits averaging 6.1% of GDP; WIFO 0.9% for Austria in 2026. In the base case Germany recovers to 1% to 1.5% as fiscal expansion and the 2028–32 tax cut take hold; Paris stays the EU's AI centre under surcharges that last to about 2028; Austria grows about 1% and cannot cut tax before 2029, remaining a liveable but expensive secondary hub that exports its scale-ups to Munich and the US; Poland, Czechia and the Baltics grow 2.5% to 3.5% on nearshored engineering; Romania consolidates below 2% to about 2027; and the US keeps California for frontier AI while Texas and Florida gain firms and people.

Three conclusions follow. Bet on US scale and European niches: defence, industrial and robotics AI, semiconductor equipment and software for regulated sectors. Watch European capital integration, not tax rates, since the EU Inc. trilogue and the capital-markets agenda say more about 2031 than any growth forecast and core tax rates will not fall before 2028–2030. Treat US talent restrictions as the largest self-inflicted risk: sustained H-1B tightening alongside scaling European programmes could make return migration Europe's first structural gain in two decades, a hypothesis to test rather than a forecast.

For an Austrian founder in 2026: keep the operating company in Austria for subsidies and research talent, take profit as dividends (44%) not salary (up to 55%), build engineering in Poland, Romania or the Baltics, raise growth rounds through Munich or US vehicles, and review the structure in 2030.

### 13 Conclusion

The gap between Europe and the United States is a gap in dynamics that the European core pays for in three currencies: labour taxes 15 to 20 points above the US, industrial electricity at three to four times the Texan price, and savings held in banks and pay-as-you-go pensions rather than equity. Owner-level capital taxes, the usual suspect, match the high-tax US states and explain little. The low-tax periphery does not escape the pattern, since its advantage is cost rather than capital and its institutions carry a risk discount.

Three things decide 2031. The EU Inc. trilogue and the capital-markets agenda determine whether European savings reach European firms. The Hormuz energy outcome determines whether the gas-price-set markets of Germany, Austria, Italy and Hungary keep their industry. The US immigration stance determines whether the one flow that has turned in Europe's favour, talent, becomes structural. Tax rates in the core will not move before 2028 to 2030, so none of this depends on them.
